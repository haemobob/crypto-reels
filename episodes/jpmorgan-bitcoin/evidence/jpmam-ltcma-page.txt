Productivity returns: An investment surge lifts growth … and volatility

In each of our annual editions after the pandemic, we highlighted increased investment, solid growth and more volatile inflation, making the case for strong returns despite geopolitical tension. In 2026, the pattern extended: A global balanced stock-bond portfolio in USD delivered a 9.1%1 total return – 220 basis points (bps) above the 25-year average.

Looking ahead, we acknowledge that volatility is inevitable over our 10- to 15-year forecast horizon as a global capex boom fuels higher growth and strong asset returns. Productivity gains mitigate rising inflation risks and an aging workforce. Despite the tail risks, a resilient economy drives resilient markets. This environment calls for a broad investment opportunity set – and raises the rewards for diversification.

This view attracts scrutiny, and rightly so. Market participants point to demographics, deficits, energy transition and geopolitical fragmentation as causes for concern. These issues are important, but they are starting conditions that define the world today. The choices made to adapt to them will define the world, and the returns, of tomorrow.

Three powerful forces shape the economy and drive returns. Economic nationalism boosts domestic investment but risks a crosscurrent of inflation volatility. Technology adoption is the fair wind filling the global economy’s sails. Rising domestic investment drives capital rewiring, resulting in shifts in public and private capital flows.

Economic nationalism2 has become entrenched as governments focus inward and spend on projects that bolster domestic resilience, at times trading economic efficiency for political expediency. Some elements of economic nationalism – industrial strategy, for instance – can be useful in reframing global issues such as climate change.3 While this can add to overall demand over time, it may also spur inflation and market volatility.

Technology adoption4 today is synonymous with artificial intelligence (AI), but breakthroughs in other areas, from medicine to robotics to space, will likely advance in the next decade, further supporting trend growth via investment and productivity. As investment intensifies, there is scope for capital rewiring.5 Former nation savers will become nation spenders.

Capital allocation ultimately steers the economy toward its destination. This has important implications for the nature and geographic spread of growth over our forecast horizon.

These themes may be disruptive, but together they boost our G76 nominal growth forecast by 30bps, to 4.2% – the best in 15 years, with the best mix of real growth and inflation since the pandemic (Exhibit 1).

Still, economies driven by investment and productivity risk becoming more unequal as gains accrue faster to capital than to labor. That inequality is central to today’s K-shaped economy,⁷ which likely persists over our investment horizon. A K-shaped economy, with unequal returns to capital vs. labor, is a metastable state – surprisingly durable, but sensitive to stress or shock. Its persistence relies on channeling sufficient economic reward to the lower leg of the K to maintain stability while optimizing the growth and profit accruing to the upper leg of the K.

Higher growth and fiscal deficits imply higher real rates and larger term risk premia. These factors, combined with starting yields that have fully normalized from the low rates of the 2010s, give us the highest global bond forecasts in two decades.

Strong investment and productivity underpin profits and equity returns, as we make a 50bps upgrade to global equity forecasts, to 7.5%. We factor prevailing risks into modestly higher volatility forecasts. For investors in a balanced portfolio, our 10- to 15-year return forecast improves 60bps, to 7.0%,8 while a balanced portfolio with 30% diversified alternatives9 sees forecasts increase 50bps, to 7.5%, with hedge funds in particular capturing the more favorable alpha environment.

In a centuries-old tale, a weary traveler asks a farmer how to get to Dublin, only to be told, “Well, I wouldn’t start from here.” The story should resonate with investors today. The cyclical starting point for their journey includes demographic challenges, high deficits, ongoing energy transition and geopolitical fragmentation.

But while these conditions define our cyclical starting point and create a hurdle, they do not derail long-term growth or asset returns. Indeed, the normalization of yields since 2022 creates a compelling entry point not just for bonds but also for other assets, including many alternatives, that have repriced relative to bonds.

Post-pandemic, governments are more willing to use fiscal levers to ignite better growth – and if corporations can be brought along for the ride, all the better. Fiscal spending by G7 nations in the 2020s has risen by 7% compared with the 2010s, and corporate capex is now growing at 6.1%, 1.4% and 1.6% year-over-year in the U.S., Europe and Japan, respectively.

Investment is a necessary precondition for lifting productivity. The McKinsey Global Institute finds that a change in investment10 explains 80% of productivity gains from 1997 to 2022. With investment rising as fiscal faucets are loosened and firms are scrambling to assert technology leadership, we see growth supported and increasingly dictated by capital deepening11 and total factor productivity (TFP).12 Even then, our productivity forecasts13 are more measured than OECD estimates.14

Higher overall growth can partially offset unequal distribution of economic gains across capital and labor, but it cannot entirely mitigate the division that fuels economic nationalist tendencies. These spending forces will influence both demand and potential supply. How this nets out will determine inflation. On average over our forecast horizon, we expect supply to keep pace and therefore inflation to remain in check, and are forecasting a global rate of 2.6%. However, we anticipate periods of supply-demand mismatch and therefore episodes of choppy inflation along the way, particularly in higher deficit nations such as the U.S. and the UK.

Economic nationalism: The good, the bad and the dangerous

This year, we dive deeper into our first theme, economic nationalism, which we have discussed in prior editions. It is a response to growing concerns over security and resilience. Economic nationalism is also a consequence of inequality15 and political polarization, creating fertile ground that more-extreme political opportunists can exploit.

Economic nationalism can be a headwind to growth, yet some aspects are certainly good housekeeping. Developing an industrial strategy can create strategic advantages, as we explore in our article on how climate change affects trend growth.16 Improving supply chains, energy security and defense can boost investment and near-term growth. Nevertheless, such spending may demand trade-offs between economic efficiency and resiliency.17

Even where intent is benign, spending driven by an agenda shaped by economic nationalism carries the risk that the investment fails to translate into productivity gains or that it crowds out other useful investments. At the more extreme end of policy choices, restrictions on trade and labor migration can directly suppress growth. On balance, we see economic nationalism as a modest growth risk but a greater risk to inflation.

In this year’s Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions, cash return forecasts are mostly stable. Meanwhile, our projection of modestly steeper curves at equilibrium, together with higher starting yields, pushes up bond return forecasts sharply, leading to global sovereign bonds offering the best return forecasts in two decades (Exhibit 2).

Many governments face high debt and persistent deficits. By contrast, corporations are often cash rich and, in public credit markets at least, quite savvy in their debt management. In short, sovereigns are perhaps not as risk free as they once were, while corporations have a shareholder return incentive to service debt responsibly. This “up in quality” trend leads us to tighten our equilibrium credit spread assumptions. While prevailing spreads are tighter than our cycle-neutral forecasts, our projections for credit returns generally keep pace with equivalent sovereign forecasts.

If economic nationalism creates crosscurrents for our economic vessel, then technology adoption is the fair wind filling its sails. We believe that AI adoption is in its early stages, but the promise of what might be possible has grown dramatically in just a few years. A historic USD 15 trillion investment boom is underway as firms seek an advantage along a fast-moving frontier. Yet we are just starting to glimpse the economy-wide potential of AI. While only 6% of U.S. work hours are AI-assisted today, we expect AI adoption to reach 40% of today’s tasks by 2040.18

AI adoption is following a contour familiar from prior innovation cycles.¹⁹ Initially, innovators capture supernormal profits and invest to build scale. Then other sectors jump in; only later are rewards shared more broadly as investments become embedded into business processes. The transition from the early to later phases is often bumpy. But over the full cycle, we see AI providing an upside risk to trend growth sufficient to more than outweigh the negative impact on labor force growth that comes from population aging and hostility to migration.

AI capex has boosted profits in regions with large technology sectors, notably the U.S., Korea and Taiwan. Over our full investment horizon, tech sector profit growth will likely moderate from current levels. But as productivity gains materialize, earnings in other sectors should rise. Korean and Taiwanese indices are dominated by cyclical technology sectors (chipmakers). Even if this tech cycle proves more durable than past ones, eventual normalization of cyclical tech profits weighs on aggregate margins, as other sectors are too small to provide an offset. Since Korea and Taiwan account for 51% of the emerging market (EM) index, our EM equity 10- 15 year forecast falls below developed markets for the first time in our publication’s history (Exhibit 3).

By contrast, the U.S. is more balanced across defensive and cyclical technology sectors, and is well diversified beyond tech. So even as tech profits normalize, we expect profits in other sectors to counteract the decline, resulting in greater aggregate margin resilience. U.S. firms also have a strong record of turning capex into returns: U.S. RoIC over the last decade averages around 17%, vs. low-teens in Europe and Japan. The result is a sizable uplift to our U.S. equity forecast, and the U.S. vs. rest of world (RoW) return differential swings in favor of the U.S., from -70bps last year to +10bps in this year’s edition.

The technology cycle also widens the opportunity set for private equity (PE) funds, partially offsetting the drag from dry powder20 and the still-challenging exit backdrop. Adoption of AI and automation is reshaping cost structures and competitive moats, in turn creating clearer dispersion between winners and losers. Together with growing competition for capital and a pipeline of dynamic innovation, these factors support the alpha share of total returns in large cap private equity – even as higher financing costs trim large cap private equity median manager returns by 60bps, to 9.6%.

Aside from PE, investment in the ecosystem enabling AI (compute, power, cooling, fiber and data centers) also supports returns across infrastructure, as well as in the communications and data-exposed segments of real estate (Exhibit 4).

Capital allocation is ultimately the rudder that steers our economic vessel. Since 2000, global savings balances21 have grown from USD 9 trillion to USD 29 trillion – in real terms, a 70% increase over 25 years; nominal pension balances22 have quadrupled, from USD 15 trillion to USD 62 trillion; and the U.S. net international investment position (NIIP)23 had swelled to USD 22.4 trillion (a net liability) by the end of 2Q 2026 (Exhibit 5).

While we do not expect this balance to reverse, with global yields having normalized post-pandemic there is simply more competition for excess capital.

As economies such as Germany deploy more of their capital at home to improve their economic resilience, this reduces the amount of excess savings seeking a home globally, with important implications for global capital flows.

Governments may also be tempted to encourage domestic capital to support domestic growth. Reforms aimed at channeling pension savings into domestic investments is one such example of a trend that could become increasingly forceful in the years ahead.

Reserve allocation24 is another key feature and a material driver of currency markets. Despite the chatter in the media, we see limited evidence of any “sell America” trends.25 Were any challenge to U.S. exceptionalism to materialize, we believe it would impact bond and FX markets far more than U.S. stocks. Simply put, as governments globally unlock domestic savings to finance local growth, marginal investment flows may be distributed more globally than in the last 15 years, when the U.S. disproportionately benefited from global capital flows.

High deficits, economic nationalism and shifts in global capital flows all point to a further decline in the trade-weighted dollar. But as expected inflation gaps between the U.S. and Europe narrow, the fair value for European crosses has fallen. By contrast, cheap valuations in Asian currencies and still-wide inflation differentials vs. the U.S. suggest that further declines in the trade-weighted dollar will be driven mainly by the South Korean won, Japanese yen and Chinese yuan (Exhibit 6).

In the same way that a cheaper USD doesn’t diminish the currency’s reserve status, the suggestion that broader currency debasement will precipitate a rush into crypto assets is also wide of the mark. The volatility and unstable correlation of assets like bitcoin (BTC) remain an obstacle to their widespread inclusion in portfolios. Nevertheless, we initiate a long-term return forecast for BTC this year while stopping short of its full inclusion in our covariance matrices. Instead, we provide a separate, smaller covariance matrix with BTC against a subset of assets in our public markets chapter.

In sum, capital rewiring may be the least immediately observable of the three themes shaping markets over the next 10 to 15 years, but for investors and asset allocators, it may well have the greatest impact on relative asset returns.

Our upbeat growth outlook may seem at odds with today’s flood of global headlines. Still, we’re constructive on returns despite the later-cycle turbulence. The key is building a resilient portfolio.

We examine the rationale for upgrading productivity forecasts and margins in the macro and public markets chapters. Still, we recognize the inherent uncertainty involved in adopting a disruptive innovation and in translating a burgeoning investment cycle into real and durable economic gains. We also acknowledge that valuations offer little cushion and that capex cycles often take a volatile final turn.

So alongside upgrading our return forecasts, we raise our volatility projections, notably in equities. Sharpe ratios are up for U.S. large cap equity, flat for EAFE and down for EM equity, where extreme cyclical margin expansion has effectively borrowed a lot from future returns (Exhibits 7A and 7B).

As a result, the relative Sharpe ratios between public and private assets tilt in favor of real assets. This reflects our economic narrative of increased competition for capital and higher inflation volatility: Alternative assets with inflation resilience, capacity for alpha capture and low correlation to public markets are an essential part of portfolios in the world we envision.

Hedge funds are winners from this new world. The higher yields we expect provide a boost to hedge funds via the carry collected on their short positions. As governments look for new ways to fund growth and pools of capital open up, opportunities for uncorrelated alpha, which hedge funds are positioned to capture, increase sharply. These supportive factors, together with improvements to our forecasting methodology, push our diversified hedge fund median manager returns up 140bps, to 6.7%, this year – with dispersion across funds offering further rewards from manager selection.

Without question, there are risks. Strong asset returns in 2026 may imply calm waters, but our themes of economic nationalism, tech adoption and capital rewiring create potent undercurrents. Our outlook implicitly projects a continuation of the K-shaped economy, which doubtless creates social (and moral) issues that policymakers must grapple with.

The greatest risk to our outlook is a miscalculation of the balance between the upper and lower legs of the K. Underappreciating the needs of the labor pool could destabilize the system. Equally, overzealous attempts to redistribute returns from capital to labor could disincentivize investment. While we expect the metastable equilibrium of the K-shaped economy to persist, factors that push the balance of rewards between capital and labor too far in either direction could cause bouts of volatility.

Other risks to our constructive outlook include restrictions on the flow of capital and widespread capital misallocation. Economic nationalism today affects labor and trade, but it has left capital flows largely untouched. Should this change, investment-led growth would surely suffer. Meanwhile, if today’s investment boom fails to boost productivity, the net effect would be lower trend growth and excess capacity that raises the risk of disinflation.

There are topside risks as well. Past innovation cycles have led to meaningful improvements in living standards. If that recurs, it might restrain some of the most damaging instincts of populism. And as AI adoption spreads, its impact can compound when it combines with other, possibly more mundane, inventions in business supply chains, creating further upside potential for corporate profits.

Portfolios: More return, more risk, a lot more opportunity

Over our 10- to 15-year investment horizon, we believe that investors can capitalize on the fair winds of solid growth and favorable asset returns while navigating the undercurrents of economic nationalism, tech adoption and capital rewiring.

In portfolios, this calls for balance and robustness. It also demands willingness to use a broad investment opportunity set. A strong margin outlook reinforces the case for U.S. equities in portfolios despite concerns over valuation and sector concentration. Still, regional diversification is essential.

Diversification with EM equities is compromised by stretched cyclical margins, which weigh heavily on return forecasts. Increasingly, however, developed market equities offer diversification options, particularly in Europe and Japan, where rising capex and investment are improving the growth outlook.

High quality bonds reflect much of the inflation volatility we anticipate in current yields. Should growth undershoot our projections, we see upside to our bond return forecasts. This in turn implies diversification potential to stocks, even allowing for positive stock-bond correlation driven by a volatile inflation environment.

A balanced stock-bond portfolio is an attractive starting point. But to navigate the themes in play, as well as uncertainty around the investment cycle and policy, alternatives can play a vital role in portfolios.

Alternatives can serve as return enhancers – adding 30% of a blend of private equity, venture capital and direct lending can boost balanced portfolio returns by 80bps and the Sharpe ratio by 11%. Alternatives can also serve as diversifiers – adding 30% of a blend of diversified hedge funds, gold, infrastructure and transportation boosts the balanced portfolio Sharpe ratio by 22% while maintaining portfolio returns (Exhibits 8A and 8B). Other alternative assets, such as real estate, can serve both to diversify and to enhance returns, given that our global core real estate forecast sits at 7.9% while global value-added real estate is at 9.3%.

Investing in a volatile world shaped by the three powerful themes we have discussed can be daunting (Exhibit 9). Focusing too much on the prevailing news cycle is distracting, but thoughtful asset allocation can keep the impulse in check. And when events heat up, a robust and diversified portfolio allows us to ask calmly: Has anything changed other than the price?

What are the J.P. Morgan Long-Term Capital Market Assumptions (LTCMAs)?

J.P. Morgan Asset Management’s annual LTCMAs provide return, risk and correlation forecasts for more than 200 major asset classes in 20-plus base currencies, over a 10- to 15-year horizon. Our LTCMAs represent the work of more than 90 of our researchers, portfolio managers and economists worldwide, using a process refined over 31 years. A publication expands on the data, analyzing trends and themes.

Designed to help pave the way to stronger portfolios, these forecasts drive J.P. Morgan Asset Management’s strategic asset allocations and power the tools we use to advise clients. They help asset allocators, financial advisors and others build portfolios, evaluate trade-offs and stress-test performance against objectives, in pursuit of their long-term goals.

We center the 2027 edition on three forces with durable implications: Economic nationalism (governments’ more domestically oriented posture); faster diffusion of technological innovation (tech adoption) and the reconfiguration of how and where public and private funds are deployed (capital rewiring). These reshape returns’ level and dispersion across regions, sectors and asset classes. With a global capex boom, they support higher growth while creating more inflation volatility.

Resilient economies support resilient markets, but with wider outcomes – raising the rewards for thoughtful diversification.

We forecast a rise in long-term returns from a balanced-plus portfolio – including alternative assets. We expect faster economic growth to boost profits and yields, lifting a balanced portfolio’s long-run returns from last year’s forecast. In equities, U.S. profit margins are more resilient than emerging markets’. In fixed income, higher real yields improve the case for government bonds, while an “up in quality” mindset and tighter spread assumptions improve our corporate credit forecast.

Higher inflation volatility, ongoing risk of currency debasement and growing opportunities to capture active alpha reinforce alternatives as essential diversifiers and potential return drivers.

We believe AI will drive a meaningful, durable uplift to productivity over 10 to 15 years. Improved productivity is the main force returning U.S. GDP growth to a faster pace over a decade. AI diffusion and the resulting productivity boost will likely be gradual, most significant in the 2030s and beyond. This is vital to our 2027 forecast. Steady uptake should limit rapid, economy-wide job displacement while giving supply chains time to adapt.
